Visualizing Training: Watching It Learn

Right now, training prints a wall of numbers scrolling down your terminal. That works, but it's hard to actually see a trend in a list of text. This lesson turns those numbers into two simple plots — loss going down, accuracy going up — and along the way teaches you how to read what those shapes are telling you.

Step 1: keep a record while you train

You already compute loss and accuracy once per epoch (Lesson 8). All you need to add is somewhere to keep them, instead of throwing them away after printing:

history = {"train_loss": [], "train_acc": [], "test_acc": []}

# inside the epoch loop, after computing loss and acc as before:
history["train_loss"].append(loss)
history["train_acc"].append(acc)

Step 2: track test performance too, not just training

This is the genuinely useful part. So far you've only checked accuracy on the test set once, at the very end. If you check it once per epoch instead — cheap, it's just one more forward pass over 10,000 images — you can watch training and test accuracy side by side as training happens.

# inside the epoch loop, alongside the training-set check:
A1_test_epoch = relu(X_test @ W1 + b1)
P_test_epoch  = softmax(A1_test_epoch @ W2 + b2)
test_acc = (P_test_epoch.argmax(axis=1) == y_test).mean()
history["test_acc"].append(test_acc)

Here's why this matters: if training accuracy keeps climbing but test accuracy stalls or starts dropping, that gap is the network starting to memorize the training images instead of learning to actually recognize digits — the exact "memorized the answer key" problem from Lesson 7. Watching both lines together is how you catch it happening, instead of finding out only at the very end.

Step 3: plot it

After training finishes, turn the recorded lists into two side-by-side charts:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from pathlib import Path

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

axes[0].plot(history["train_loss"])
axes[0].set_title("Training Loss")
axes[0].set_xlabel("epoch")
axes[0].set_ylabel("loss")

axes[1].plot(history["train_acc"], label="train")
axes[1].plot(history["test_acc"], label="test")
axes[1].set_title("Accuracy")
axes[1].set_xlabel("epoch")
axes[1].set_ylabel("accuracy")
axes[1].legend()

plt.tight_layout()
out_path = Path(__file__).parent / "training_curves.png"
plt.savefig(out_path)
print("saved training curves to", out_path)

Reading the shapes

Once you can see the curves, they tell you things a printed number never could:

What you seeWhat it usually means
Loss drops smoothly, then flattenstraining is working as intended
Loss barely moves at alllearning rate too low, or training has already converged
Loss jumps around wildly, spikylearning rate too high (Lesson 6)
Train accuracy much higher than testoverfitting — the network is starting to memorize
Train and test accuracy close together, but both lowunderfitting — the network needs more capacity (a bigger hidden layer) or more epochs
Primary source CS231n: Neural Networks Part 3 — "Babysitting the learning process" This section is built entirely around reading exactly these two plots: what different loss-curve shapes reveal about your learning rate, and what the gap between training and validation accuracy reveals about overfitting. It's the standard reference for this skill.
